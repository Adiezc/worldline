# Simulation rules: Modern Day (2026)

**Realism first.** Outcomes follow how the world actually works in 2026. A single turn rarely changes much. Big shifts need sustained effort, allies and luck.

**Nuclear deterrence.** Nine states have nuclear weapons. No one invades a nuclear power's core territory or tries to topple its regime by force without facing escalation risks that leaders treat as unacceptable. Nuclear powers fight through proxies, sanctions, cyber operations and limited strikes. Any nuclear use is a world-changing event with enormous political, economic and humanitarian consequences.

**Wars are slow and costly.** Modern militaries with drones, missiles and dense defences advance slowly, often only a few kilometres a month. Offensives burn equipment, ammunition and manpower. War exhaustion rises, economies suffer, and outside powers resupply the weaker side. Quick, decisive conquest is the exception and usually requires overwhelming superiority against a weak, isolated target.

**Economic interdependence.** Supply chains (chips, rare earths, energy, food) bind rivals together. Tariffs, export controls and sanctions hurt the imposer as well as the target. Markets react within days, and capital flight, currency crises and inflation follow reckless policy.

**Sanctions bite gradually.** They raise costs and erode growth over months and years. They rarely change a regime's behaviour quickly, and evasion through third countries is common. Coalitions matter more than unilateral measures.

**Domestic politics matter.** Elections, coalition arithmetic, courts, protests and elite factions constrain every government. Democracies face scheduled elections that can replace leaders and change course. Autocracies fear unrest, elite splits and economic collapse. Unpopular wars and austerity cost support.

**Diplomacy and institutions.** Alliances, treaties and organisations shape behaviour. The UN Security Council is limited by vetoes. EU and NATO decisions need consensus or large majorities. Leaders weigh credibility, reputation and precedent.

**Information environment.** Disinformation, social media and leaks shape opinion quickly. Atrocities and blunders are seen worldwide. Narratives can rally or erode support.

**Plausibility.** Countries act according to their interests, capabilities and leaders' personalities. Surprises happen, such as coups, disasters, assassinations and market crashes, but they should be rooted in existing tensions.
