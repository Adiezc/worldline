# Simulation rules: Cold War (October 1962)

**Realism first.** Outcomes follow how the world worked in 1962. A single turn rarely changes much. Big shifts need sustained effort, allies and luck.

**Nuclear brinkmanship.** The United States, the Soviet Union, Britain and France have nuclear weapons. China is working on a bomb. America's strategic lead is large but cannot prevent catastrophic damage to itself or Europe. Leaders bluff, threaten and probe, and confrontations move up a ladder: warnings, alerts, blockades, mobilisation. Both sides fear miscalculation. An attack on a superpower's forces or on a protected ally risks escalation that no rational leader wants. Any nuclear use is a world-changing catastrophe with immense casualties and political consequences.

**Two blocs and the non-aligned.** NATO and the Warsaw Pact face each other in Germany. Bloc members follow their leader on core security questions, though de Gaulle, Romania and others seek more room. Moscow will use force to keep Eastern Europe in line, as in Hungary in 1956. Washington backs friendly dictators against communist movements and will intervene, openly or covertly, in its hemisphere. The non-aligned states play the superpowers against each other for aid and arms, and their votes decide the UN General Assembly.

**The communist schism.** The Sino-Soviet split is real and deepening. China and the Soviet Union compete for the loyalty of communist parties, liberation movements and the Third World. Albania sides with Beijing, while North Korea and North Vietnam balance between the two.

**Decolonisation.** The empires are being wound up. Britain negotiates independence colony by colony and usually transfers power peacefully, except where settlers resist (Kenya, the Rhodesias). Portugal and South Africa fight to hold on. New states are fragile: coups, secessions, army mutinies and one-party rule are common, and the superpowers court them with aid. When a colony gains independence, the colonial power's provinces there pass to a new state.

**Proxy wars and insurgency.** Guerrilla wars (Vietnam, Laos, Angola, Yemen, the Congo) are slow and politically costly. Conventional armies struggle against insurgents who have sanctuaries and outside support. Superpowers prefer advisers, arms and covert action to sending combat troops.

**Wars are costly.** Mid-century armies can move fast against weak opponents, but wars between serious powers are bloody and draw in allies. War exhaustion rises, economies suffer and outside powers resupply the weaker side.

**Limited information.** There is no internet and no satellite television news. Intelligence comes from spies, diplomats, radio intercepts, U-2 flights and early reconnaissance satellites. It is slow, partial and often wrong. Governments can keep secrets for weeks, deny deployments, and are surprised by events. Communications between capitals take hours, and there is no hotline between Washington and Moscow. Leaders can misread each other's intentions.

**Economics.** The Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates and the dollar anchors the West. Western Europe and Japan are growing fast, the Soviet bloc grows through central planning, and much of the Third World depends on commodity exports. Trade between the blocs is small and controlled (CoCom). Embargoes hurt small targets like Cuba badly but do little to the Soviet Union.

**Domestic politics matter.** Democracies hold scheduled elections and suffer scandals and strikes. Military coups are a normal way to change government in Latin America, Africa, the Middle East and Asia. Communist leaders face Politburo intrigue rather than voters. Racial segregation in the US South and apartheid in South Africa shape both domestic and international politics.

**Diplomacy and institutions.** The UN Security Council is paralysed by vetoes on Cold War issues. The Republic of China holds China's seat. The General Assembly is a stage for anti-colonial majorities. NATO decisions need consensus. Summits are rare, formal events.

**Plausibility.** Countries act according to their interests, capabilities and leaders' personalities. Surprises happen, such as coups, assassinations, defections, uprisings and accidents, but they should be rooted in existing tensions. Avoid hindsight: no one in 1962 knows how the Cold War will end.
